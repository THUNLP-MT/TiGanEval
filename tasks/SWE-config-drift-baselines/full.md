Complete the estate's compliance service so that it keeps a durable record ledger, gives the operator a queue of `detected` rows she can work down to nothing without losing acknowledged and ignored decisions from the ledger, and makes the number on each environment mean the same thing from one Monday to the next. The console runs recorded under `project_records/` are examples of what the service sees, not an exhaustive list of the situations the finished service has to handle.

A deviation is a stored record with an identity: one baseline, one service, one drift type and one field, which is what `protocol.record_key` is made of. A night's work is a reconciliation of the conditions the estate is in against the records the baseline in force already holds, not an append: a condition that was true last night and is true again tonight stays the record it was, with the night it was first seen on, while its `expected` and `actual` are refreshed to the canonical values from the current night; a condition that is newly true opens one record. A record is per changed setting rather than per service, through `protocol.drift_type_for` and `protocol.field_for`, because a service whose image was bumped and whose labels were edited is two deviations with two severities that go to two different people.

Both sides of every comparison go through `protocol.canonical_config` first. Env entries, port bindings and mounts are contents rather than sequences, so a host that comes back after a restart reporting the same things in another order is not a change; labels compare by key. A setting the collector did not report on is not a change to nothing, and produces no record at all. What a record stores for its current two sides is the canonical form written out with `protocol.render_value`, so the same canonical value always reads the same way. For a `service_missing` or `service_added` record, the side on which the service exists stores the value selected from its canonical config by `protocol.RELEASE_REFERENCE_KEY`, rendered the same way, and the side on which it does not exist stores the empty string.

Records have a lifecycle that survives the next night. A record in `detected` whose condition is no longer among the conditions this night found becomes `resolved`, stamped once with that night's clock reading and not stamped again afterwards; a record whose condition is still among them is left open, because a row leaves the list on the night the thing it describes stops being true and not before. A record the operator moved to `acknowledged` or `ignored` is left alone: it is never resolved by the service, and while its condition still holds it never gives rise to a second open record, because those decisions are the only thing the operator puts into the service and they have to stay put. A condition that clears and later becomes true again opens one new record rather than reviving the resolved one.

The night's numbers are taken over the population the baseline declares. `total_services` is the number of services in the baseline in force, whatever the scan happened to contain. That population is partitioned into four groups: `missing_services` are absent from the scan; `drifted_services` have at least one reported setting that differs; `unknown_services` are present and have no confirmed deviation but omit at least one setting declared by the baseline; and `compliant_services` are present, fully observed against the declaration and unchanged. A confirmed deviation takes precedence over omitted settings, so each baseline service belongs to exactly one group and the four counts add up to `total_services`. A baseline service the scan does not hold produces a `service_missing` record. A scanned service the baseline does not declare produces a `service_added` record, counted in `added_services` outside that population. The four severity counters count the conditions found against the active baseline this night, in whatever status their durable records stand and whichever night those records were opened on. They do not count every record retained in the ledger or only the `detected` operator queue. The score is `protocol.compliance_score` of the compliant count and the total, which reads 100.0 for an environment whose baseline declares nothing.

Baseline state is single-valued and auditable. Capturing a baseline and activating one both leave exactly one baseline in force in that environment and put any other in that environment out of force. A baseline put out of force keeps everything written against it: it is not compared against while another is in force, and when it is put back in force a condition its records already hold is still that record, on the night it was first seen. A scan of an environment with nothing in force raises `protocol.NoActiveBaseline` and leaves behind no numbers and no point in the history, because an environment reported as compliant against nothing at all is an environment nobody will look at. Deleting a baseline removes the drift records and the nightly numbers written against that baseline, and reaches nothing written against any other baseline or environment.

`estate/reconcile.py` and `estate/notes.py` are the files to change. `estate/protocol.py` ships with the platform release in every region. You can inspect the project, replay the recorded runs and run your own experiments before you deliver.
