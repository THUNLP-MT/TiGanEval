Finish the Northgate readout and hand it back as a notebook the next market can run unchanged. The council takes one number out of it: the observed sixty-day ordering difference between the accounts the line offered and the ones it passed over, with a range under it and the account-days that range stands on. It is the campaign comparison at the line, not a counterfactual claim about what the same account would have done under the other offer decision.

The accounts the line decides are the ones the weekly run scored. An account the run carried is on neither side of anything: no offer went to it and no offer was withheld from it by the line, so it belongs in neither the offered rows nor the ones passed over. And the accounts the line decides are the ones near it. Read each side off the rows within five points of the line either way and never across the whole book; the book above the line was always going to order more than the book below it, and that difference is not the offer's.

The side an account is on is the side the run put it on, which is not always the side the register shows today. The register carries whatever the last refresh left. Where an account appears in the recompute log with a refresh dated after the day its offer went out, the score the run read is the `score_before` on that log row, and that is the score that decides which side it is on and whether it is inside the five points. Where the refresh landed before its run, the register's own score is the one the run read. Batches R-1 and R-2 landed on 3 and 9 April here and the runs go out every Monday from 2 March, so this cuts both ways inside one window.

A credit is a manager's, not the line's. Where the credit filed against an account carried it from under the line to over it — its score less its `credits_filed` would have been under 61.0 and its score is not — the line did not decide that account and it does not belong in the reading. The score in that test is the score the run read, the same one the side is taken from, and not the one the register carries today. Leave those rows out and count them. An account with no credit, or one whose credit was not enough to carry it over, stays in. The ceiling on a credit is in the campaign's own terms, `credit_ceiling`, so the rows this takes out are the ones inside that much of the line and no wider; it is one point here and it is not one point everywhere.

Write the answer in points of sixty-day order rate, the offered side less the side passed over, so a number that helps the offer is positive.

Sixty days is not what the export watched. It stops on 24 May and an order reaches it two days after it happens, so an account offered after 23 March was never watched for its full sixty and the later runs are the ones the line was easiest to clear. Count each account over the days it was actually watched — from its offer to its first order where the export carries one, and to its `last_observed_date` where it does not — and take each side's orders over its own account-days. What that gives you is a rate per account-day, and the number the council prices is a share of accounts: read the rate back as the chance that an account watched at it orders at least once in sixty days. Sixty times the daily rate is not that. It is the orders sixty days would bring in, it runs above the share of accounts behind them, and it runs further above on the side that orders more often, so it moves the difference as well as the two rates. A file the desk has since reviewed is watched to today rather than to the export's stop, so count that file on the days the review gives it rather than on the days the export gave it.
