Finish the bushing qualification and hand it back as a notebook the next campaign can run unchanged. Purchasing takes one number out of it: supplier B's wear rate against supplier A's, as the two would read on rig 1's fixture with a calibration signed off that morning. They will act on it if it is right to within four parts in a hundred, and they want a range printed with it that holds the true rate.

The load cell reads higher the longer a rig has run since the calibration house last signed it off, and it reads back at what it should the morning after a visit. The certified block that goes on at the top of most sessions is clamped straight onto that cell, so what the block reads at those hours is what the cell was doing to every reading taken at those hours on that rig. What it does is a share of the reading and not a fixed number of microns: a block that comes back a quarter over its certified twenty means a part taken on that rig at those hours came back a quarter over what the part really was, deep wear and shallow alike. Each rig carries its own certificate and the house comes to the two on separate visits, so the hours behind a reading are the hours its own rig has run under its own certificate: rig 1 ran the first eighty-odd hours of this campaign on one certificate, longer than either rig has gone on one since. The bench sheet says which certificate every session ran under and the booking terms say what a part session and a block run each cost in hours, so those hours can be counted off the table itself.

Rig 2 holds the part in a wider seat than rig 1 does and reads lower for the same part because of it. The block is clamped in neither seat, so nothing the block reads says how much lower; the only thing that says it is a lot with parts on both rigs, and this campaign has two, A-3330 and B-7122. The next campaign will have its own two, so the notebook has to pick them out of the table it is handed rather than know them. Read the gap on each of them once each reading is already back on its own rig's fresh calibration, and take the two together.

Supplier B's lots all landed after rig 2 came online, so thirty-five of the forty-six B parts sit on rig 2 and forty-three of the forty-eight A parts sit on rig 1. The review is asking about the two suppliers and not about the two rigs, and with the sides split like that neither the calibration nor the seat comes off by itself; both have to be off a reading before the two suppliers are put beside each other.

Parts out of one lot are alike in a way that parts out of two lots are not, so what the campaign really has is six A lots and five B lots and not ninety-four sessions. Give each lot one vote on the log-ratio scale. Build the range on the eleven lots. It must not come out narrower than eleven lots allow, it must not be so wide that it says less than those eleven lots already say, and it must hold the true rate.

Twelve bench-hours were set aside for the campaign before the review on day 85, and the console has already charged two block runs against them. A part session is two hours and a block run is half an hour. Rig 2 is out for its fixture pin from the 81st to the 83rd, so no more than eight of those hours can go on rig 2. A rig's own window is spent the same way the twelve are: an hour the console has already charged on a rig is an hour that rig no longer has open. The booking terms carry the twelve and each rig's window for whichever campaign the notebook is run on. Stores holds eleven spares, six A and five B, and a part is worn once.

Everything the campaign can still settle about the two suppliers is settled by the parts already run, except how much lower rig 2 reads for the same part. A part can still sharpen that comparison when it puts its lot on the other rig or strengthens the thinner side of a lot already split across both. Book the hours on those. Stop when no spare left in stores would add anything, or when the hours will not pay for another session.

Every session the console has charged against those twelve hours appears in the report once, with the run id the console gave it, the rig it ran on, whether it was a part or the block, and the reading it returned, as it came back and not as the notebook has since put it on another scale. The bench sheet's own rows were paid for out of the campaign's earlier hours and belong on no line of it. A block run is not a part session; it belongs in neither supplier's session count. Every valid part-session reading in the table or receipt stream stands behind the comparison and its supplier count.

`analysis.py` and `support.py` are the files to change. `protocol.py` is the shape the reporting stack reads a notebook through and ships with the stack. The same notebook runs the bench's other qualification campaigns on their own certificates, their own seats, their own lots and their own hours, so nothing in it should be written for this campaign's numbers.
