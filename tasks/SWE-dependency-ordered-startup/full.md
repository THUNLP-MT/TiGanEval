Complete the API service's start-up so that a deploy brings the stack up in the order the application actually needs, inside the limits the platform sets, and so that a deploy that fails leaves the process holding nothing. The start-ups recorded under `project_records/` are examples of what the console drives, not an exhaustive list of the manifests the finished `bring_up()` has to handle.

A service that has a connect step is connected only after every service it is built on has finished connecting. What it is built on comes from the edges each registration declares, through `Registry.declared_dependencies`, which answers for every service whether or not that service has a connect step of its own; an object that only holds a pool still stands between the pool and everything that reads through it. So the ordering is computed over the whole declared manifest, and only the services that carry a connect step are scheduled, keeping the position the whole manifest gives them. This holds whatever order the console happens to register the manifest in and whatever `max_parallel_starts` is set to on the host.

At most `max_parallel_starts` connect steps are in flight at any moment of the boot, because the warehouse handshake service refuses a fifth in-flight connection from one client. Within that limit the boot uses the parallelism it can: the whole stack is connected within `deploy_window_ticks` of the call, which the serial boot the process does today is not.

Each connect step runs at most once in the life of a process. A second `bring_up()` after a successful one schedules nothing and returns. After a connect step has failed, `bring_up()` schedules nothing further until something explicitly resets the start-up, because the connect steps would run again against hosts that were just given their resources back and some of those hosts open one session per client process, so the second attempt is worse than a stopped deploy. A manifest that cannot be brought up at all — a cycle, or an edge to a name nobody registered — is rejected before anything is connected, and rejecting it leaves the start-up able to run: the operator corrects the edge in place and calls `bring_up()` again in the same process, and that run connects every service exactly once.

What the registry hands a caller is the connected object and nothing else. A service with a connect step is not published until its own connect step has completed, so the health endpoint and the request handlers that resolve during the boot are refused rather than given a constructed but unconnected object that answers plausibly and wrongly. A service with no connect step stays resolvable throughout, since settings and feature flags are what the boot itself reads. When a connect step returns a value, that value replaces the registered object for every later caller, so what a dependent and every later request handler are given is the connected wrapper rather than the object the console registered; a connect step that returns nothing leaves the registered object in place. After the boot, `Registry.resolve(name)` and the object the ledger recorded at that service's `start_end` are the same object.

When a connect step fails, every connect step already launched alongside it is allowed to reach its end before the teardown begins, because a step that has been launched belongs to the deploy loop and will take its handle whether or not anything is waiting for it. The teardown then stops exactly the services whose connect step completed, each of them once, and never stops a service before something built on that service; `shutdown_all` is the process-exit helper and does neither of those things. An error raised by a stop step is noted and swallowed, so what the deploy log ends on is the failure that actually stopped the deploy, naming the service whose connect step could not finish. Afterwards `Ledger.open_handles()` is empty.

`bootstrap/startup.py` and `bootstrap/planner.py` are the files to change. `bootstrap/contract.py` ships in the platform release. You can read the project, replay the recorded start-ups and run your own experiments before you deliver.
